# LITRPG.md

Genre reference for litfire. This file exists for two audiences: a human contributor who has never read a LitRPG, and the LLM agents that ground their work in it. It is opinionated on purpose — a hedged description of a genre is useless as context.

---

## 1. What LitRPG is

LitRPG — Literary Role-Playing Game — is fiction in which the rules of a game are visible inside the story and binding on it. Characters have levels, stats, skills, and experience points, and those numbers appear on the page. When a character gets stronger, the reader is told by how much.

The genre formalized in Russia in the early 2010s. The label was coined in late 2013 in a brainstorming session at EKSMO, Russia's largest publisher, involving Vasily Mahanenko and colleagues; EKSMO began publishing under it as a branded category that same year. The early canon — Mahanenko's _The Way of the Shaman_, Dmitry Rus's _Play to Live: AlterWorld_, Dem Mikhailov's _Clan Dominance_ — reached English readers through translation around 2015. Royal Road, launched in 2013, became the Western incubator, and by the late 2010s English-original LitRPG was a dominant force in self-published fantasy.

Russia's real contribution was less invention than infrastructure: it built the shelf. Once a reader could find the category, the category could stabilize expectations — and this genre runs on expectations more than most.

### The boundary

**GameLit** is the umbrella: any fiction where games are central. **LitRPG** is the subset where the mechanics are explicit and trackable. A character who is simply very good at fighting is not LitRPG. A character who levels up and the reader watches the number change is.

**Progression fantasy** is the adjacent category — fiction organized around a character becoming measurably more capable. Cultivation novels are progression fantasy without a System. Most LitRPG is progression fantasy; not all progression fantasy is LitRPG.

The operative test: **remove the numbers. If the story survives intact, it wasn't LitRPG.**

## 2. The System

The System is the game-like framework governing the world — what is possible, what is difficult, what is forbidden. It is the skeleton, and it is the single element that most determines whether a book works.

A System is not a stat sheet. A stat sheet is an artifact the System produces.

### What makes a System good

**It has an author.** Someone or something built this thing. What did they want? A System that is merely ambient physics is inert; a System with intent — a bureaucracy, a business, a god, an audience — generates plot by itself. The best modern LitRPG treats the System as a character.

**It has hard limits.** Magic systems are made interesting by their ceilings, not their powers. What is definitively impossible? The limit should be inconvenient for the protagonist in a way the story keeps running into.

**It has costs.** What does advancement take that a person does not get back? "It's dangerous" is not a cost. Time, memory, relationships, humanity, options — a System where getting stronger is purely upside has no tension in its central activity.

**Its choices are not obvious.** If every reader can see that the legendary skill beats the common one, the choice is not a choice. The genre's best moments come from the apparently wrong pick turning out brilliant because of how the character used it. Design choices with genuine tradeoffs, or accept that build decisions will be dead scenes.

**It has an exploit.** What is the obvious abuse a clever person would find? Has the System closed it, and if so, why and by whom? An author who has not thought about the exploit has not finished the System, because the readers will find it in chapter three.

**The world predates the interface.** A common failure is a game world that feels like it was built five minutes before the protagonist logged in. Depth here — history, other players, a sense that the rules have a past — is most of what separates a memorable System from a competent one.

## 3. The reader contract

LitRPG readers are unusually engaged and unusually specific about what they want. They build wikis. They argue about build optimization. They notice when a skill described one way in chapter five works differently in chapter forty. Consistency is not a craft preference in this genre; it is a survival requirement.

What they show up for:

**Measurable growth.** The pleasure is watching a number go up and knowing it means something. Progress must be legible.

**Frequent payoff.** Pacing expectations are fast. Long stretches without advancement, revelation, or a system event lose readers. This is a genre that grew up on serialized web fiction, and the chapter-level rhythm reflects it.

**Fair rules.** The reader must believe the System is consistent, even where it is not fully explained. Victories that violate established rules feel like cheating in a way they don't in soft-magic fantasy.

**Competence and its earning.** The protagonist should be good at things and should have visibly become so.

**Legibility of state.** Readers should always roughly know where the protagonist stands — level, condition, capability. Withholding this entirely is disorienting.

They tolerate a great deal — rough prose, thin secondaries, absurd premises — and almost nothing in the way of broken math.

## 4. Forms

- **VRMMO / trapped-in-game** — the original shape. Players enter a virtual world; often they cannot leave.
- **System Apocalypse** — the System arrives on Earth and rewrites reality. Currently dominant. _Dungeon Crawler Carl_ is the standout.
- **Isekai / portal** — transported to a world that runs on game rules.
- **Dungeon core / base builder** — the protagonist is the dungeon, or builds one.
- **Tower climb** — ascending floors of escalating difficulty, each with its own rules.

The form determines the ambient question: _how do I get out_ (VRMMO), _how do we survive_ (apocalypse), _how do I grow_ (tower), _how do I build_ (core).

## 5. Craft — the failure modes

Every one of these is common enough to be worth naming explicitly.

### Spreadsheet fiction

The named disease of the genre. The world-building becomes the story, and the reader is wading through skill descriptions waiting for something to happen. Symptoms: stat blocks after every minor encounter, multi-page skill trees, formula explanations in the narrative body.

The fix is not fewer mechanics — it's mechanics with consequences. **Show impact, not just deltas.** "Strength +3" is a receipt. "He swung harder than before, and the goblin left the ground" is a scene. Ideally both, in that order, with the number confirming what the prose already made you feel.

Where numbers genuinely need explaining — formulas, full tables — the convention is an appendix or an author's note, not the chapter.

### Level-driven structure

Books plotted as "levels 1–10 in book one, 10–20 in book two" sag in the middle, reliably. A dungeon crawl is a setting, not a plot. The plot is what the character wants, what stands in the way, and what changes.

**Structure on narrative arcs; let progression serve them.** The right question for an arc is what it is about emotionally, then what power growth supports that. A boss fight timed to a level threshold is good design; a level threshold that exists because it's time for one is not.

### Status blocks as pacing

Where and how often stat blocks appear is a craft decision with real pacing consequences. The workable default: after significant conflicts or genuine milestones, not after every skirmish. Keep them tight. A block that a reader skims is a block that cost you nothing but also bought you nothing.

### Progression drift

Two directions, both fatal. Level too fast and threats stop mattering — the reader loses the sense that anything is at risk. Level too slow and the reader loses the genre's core pleasure. Both are arithmetic problems, and both are visible on a chart long before they're visible in prose.

### Contradiction

The one readers punish hardest. A skill that changes behavior, a stat that was one thing and is now another, an item acquired twice. In a 200,000-word series this is a bookkeeping problem, not a talent problem — which is precisely why it should be handled by software.

## 6. The authoring system

Distilled to a process, with how litfire supports each step.

**1. Build the System first.** Treat the rules like a hard magic system: define limits before powers. Establish stats, curves, and how abilities are acquired. Know your ceiling and your cost. Know your exploit.
→ `/system` interviews for meaning first, mechanics second. Formulas are executable, so curves are real rather than aspirational.

**2. Lay the backbone.** Major world events as fixed anchors; arcs as the spans between them. Each arc gets an intended power milestone alongside its narrative purpose.
→ `/timeline` produces world events and ordered arcs with milestones. Arcs are the ordering unit for everything downstream.

**3. Know what it's about.** Themes with an antagonist, stated as arguments rather than topics. Not "survival" — "survival is sold back to you at a markup."
→ `/themes` builds two-level pillars with explicit tension poles, and reports coverage without ever enforcing it.

**4. Write freely.** Scenes, in whatever order they arrive, without solving the timeline first. This is the part that is supposed to be fun.
→ Situations carry declared ledger events in frontmatter and may sit unplaced indefinitely.

**5. Let the math be checked, not remembered.** Progression drift, prerequisite violations, milestone gaps — arithmetic, computed on every save.
→ Replay produces state; deviations become open questions that never block.

**6. Assemble.** Order, reconcile, and connect into chapters, preserving the spirit of each scene.
→ Slice 2.

## 7. Working vocabulary

**System** — the rule framework governing the world. **Status screen / stat block** — the rendered display of a character's state. **System message** — an in-world notification (level up, quest, achievement). **Build** — a character's chosen configuration of stats and skills. **Grinding** — repetitive activity for incremental gain. **Milestone** — a threshold unlocking new capability. **Drop** — an item or ability gained from a defeated enemy. **Class** — a role determining available progression. **Floor / tier / rank** — ascending difficulty bands. **Debuff / buff** — temporary modifiers. **Min-maxing** — optimizing a build to extremes.

## 8. Principles for agents

For LLM agents working in this repo:

- **Numbers are checkable; story is not.** Enforce arithmetic. Never enforce theme, tone, or quality.
- **Surface contradictions; never adjudicate them.** Report that two things disagree. The author decides which is true.
- **The System means something.** When helping an author develop mechanics, always route through purpose, cost, and limit before reaching for numbers.
- **Never reach for the genre default.** No chosen ones, no ancient prophecies, no mysterious guiding voices unless the author established them.
- **Impact over receipts.** When suggesting how a mechanic appears on the page, prefer consequence in the world to a stat line.
- **Assume an expert reader.** Anything inconsistent will be noticed by someone who has read four hundred of these.
